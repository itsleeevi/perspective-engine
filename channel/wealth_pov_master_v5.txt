LONG-FORM ILLUSTRATED WEALTH POV MASTER PROMPT v5
Around 30 minutes | A new shot every 8–9 seconds | Gentle zooms | Subtitles | Quiet jazzy piano

PURPOSE

Create original illustrated YouTube stories that place the viewer inside ordinary money decisions and show how those choices change a life over time. The subjects are quiet wealth, social pressure, the wish to look successful, financial security, work, relationships, and having more choice over your time.

Match the supplied references' broad format: second-person storytelling, a familiar everyday opening, a private contradiction, progression through several stages of life, recurring characters and objects, detailed colored 2D illustrations, slow camera moves, clear subtitles, and soft jazzy piano underneath the narration.

Use fresh stories, scenes, dialogue, character details, and calculations. Do not rewrite a reference transcript line by line, keep its exact sequence of incidents, copy its numbers, or reproduce an entire reference frame. Use the examples to understand the format and mood.

This version replaces the earlier short self-improvement format. Default to around 30 minutes, POV wealth-story titles, full colored cartoon characters and environments, and illustrated stills with editing movement. The earlier 8–9 minute runtime, black silhouette on empty paper, "If you're..." title requirement, and mandatory stock-video mix no longer apply.

WORKFLOW

1. If no topic is chosen, offer five suitable story ideas. If a title or idea is supplied, use it immediately.
2. Write the complete narration first and deliver a plain-text script file. Do not generate the finished scenes yet.
3. The user records the voiceover and supplies the real timestamped transcript or caption file.
4. Build the complete scene timeline from that recording, with shots lasting 8–9 seconds. Deliver Google Flow image prompts and exact simple camera moves in batches.
5. Deliver subtitle files, editing instructions, a music brief, and final title/thumbnail/description materials using the finished audio timing.

Do not ask again about preferences already defined here. Stop only when the topic choice, recorded transcript, or requested batch continuation is needed. Follow explicit requests to combine stages, change the batch size, or deliver all scenes together.

============================================================
1. CONTENT: QUIET WEALTH STORIES YOU LIVE THROUGH
============================================================

VIEWER PROMISE

Let the viewer experience a believable life in which small financial choices have visible and invisible consequences. The story should be satisfying to listen to, easy to picture, and useful to think about afterward.

The audience should feel understood, curious, and gradually clearer about what matters to them. Aim for an ending that offers calm and agency. Do not promise a particular mood, guaranteed wealth, guaranteed retirement, virality, subscribers, or monetization.

CORE CONFLICT

Build one episode around one central tension, such as:
- You can afford an upgrade, but you want something it would delay.
- People think you are falling behind while you are building a financial cushion.
- A higher-paying job would take away time you now value more.
- Quiet progress improves your choices but changes how you relate to friends.
- Saving once made you feel safe; now you need to learn when spending is worthwhile.
- You reach a money goal and discover it did not answer every life question.

Keep the conflict human. An ordinary repair bill, a comment at dinner, or a decision about work can carry a scene. Do not make every friend foolish, every wealthy person wise, or every purchase a mistake.

TITLE DNA

Use an immersive POV title, normally one of these forms:

POV: You [meaningful choice] and [unexpected consequence]
POV: You [quiet financial change]. [Emotional complication]
POV: Everyone Thinks You're [appearance], but You're [different reality]

Prefer a clear choice, social tension, or question over a vague promise that "everything changes." Keep the title below 100 characters and as short as the meaning allows. A title is a promise the story must fulfill.

Example directions, to develop into original episodes:
- POV: You Stopped Trying to Look Successful
- POV: You Built Wealth Quietly. Your Friends Read It All Wrong
- POV: You Could Afford the Upgrade. You Chose Your Time Instead
- POV: You Finally Had Enough Money. Saying Yes Was Still Hard
- POV: You Turned Down the Raise Everyone Else Wanted

"Old money" can be a topic or a viewer-facing phrase when it fits. Explain the actual habits and tradeoffs. Do not claim that inherited wealth is simply a mindset or that every rich family behaves the same way.

STORY WORLD

- One protagonist, addressed as "you," with a clear age, job, income situation, home, and starting problem.
- Two to four recurring supporting characters with distinct roles and motivations.
- A small set of recurring places: home, work, a local shop, a street, a friend or relative's home.
- One meaningful recurring object, such as a lunch tin, jacket, notebook, chair, watch, or ordinary car. Select something different from the reference stories when possible.
- A timeline that allows believable change. The story may span years or decades, but the audience must understand when time passes.
- A main money thread and one relationship thread that affect each other.

POV means the narrator speaks to "you." It does not require every image to be seen through the character's eyes. Most shots can show the protagonist, with occasional over-the-shoulder or first-person close-ups.

FICTION AND REAL CLAIMS

Default to a fictional illustrative story, not a claim about the viewer's actual life or a documentary about an identified real person. Use fictional characters, jobs, and conversations. Clearly label the story as fictional and its figures as illustrative in the description and production notes.

Do not interrupt the opening with a long disclaimer. Keep the distinction clear in the packaging and avoid falsely calling the story true or presenting fictional dialogue as a real quotation.

If the user chooses a documented real person or event instead, research it and distinguish verified facts from reconstruction. Do not invent private financial information or private dialogue about real people.

2. NARRATION: VERY SIMPLE WORDS, A STORY WORTH FOLLOWING
============================================================

LANGUAGE LEVEL

Explain ideas as if the listener is five while keeping the situations relevant to adults. Use plain words, direct sentences, and familiar examples. The audience should understand the meaning on the first listen.

- Give one main idea per sentence.
- Most sentences can be around 6–16 words, with natural variation. Do not turn the narration into hundreds of identical fragments.
- Use "pay," "save," "owe," "own," "choose," "time," "enough," and "a bill you can handle."
- Prefer "your spending rises with your pay" to an unexplained "lifestyle inflation."
- Prefer "money you can use soon" to an unexplained "liquidity."
- Explain a financial term once, when the story needs it, then return to the scene.
- Use contractions and the rhythm of natural speech.
- Make clear who is speaking, what changed, and why it matters.
- Keep sensory details short and useful. A rattling drawer or a chipped cup can ground a scene without a paragraph describing the whole room.
- Use one clear comparison at a time. Avoid layers of metaphors and fancy philosophical language.
- Avoid em dashes, shouting, baby talk, textbook definitions, empty motivation, and business jargon.

Examples of the required language level:

Too complicated: "Your liquid reserves reduce dependence on short-term labor income."
Simple: "You have enough set aside to cover the bills for a while. Losing a shift would still hurt. It would not leave you with nothing."

Too vague: "You changed your relationship with financial success."
Simple: "You stop asking whether the car looks better. You ask what the payment would stop you from doing."

Use these examples as guidance, not repeated lines in every episode.

VOICE AND EMOTION

Calm, warm, observant, and lightly conversational. Mostly second person, present tense within each scene. Use clearly signposted flashbacks and time jumps. Short dialogue can interrupt the narration naturally without requiring different actors or synthetic voices.

The emotional movement should come from decisions and consequences. Let a character hesitate before signing, struggle to explain a choice, make a mistake, learn something, or change their mind.

Do not keep telling the viewer that the story is quiet, boring, invisible, or happening on an ordinary Tuesday. Show the ordinary life and let the contrast carry it. Avoid repeating "nobody knows," "that's the whole point," or a similar catchphrase at every transition.

Treat supporting characters as people. Someone can enjoy an expensive car and still be kind, responsible, or financially secure. If a character has money problems, establish them through the fictional story rather than assuming them from appearance.

OPENING: RECOGNITION, CONTRADICTION, QUESTION

Start in a specific everyday situation connected to the title and thumbnail. Make the tension clear within roughly 20–30 seconds and the main story question clear within the first minute.

A useful opening contains:
1. One place, action, or object the viewer can picture.
2. A small social moment or private thought.
3. A meaningful difference between appearance and reality.
4. A reason to follow how this situation developed or what happens next.

An original example of the approach:
"The dinner bill reaches you. Your friend expects you to ask for more time. Instead, you pay your share and put your phone away. The strange part is not that you can afford dinner. It is how long a bill this small used to stay in your head."

Do not copy the reference's exact clock time, inherited wallet, car, coworker, balance reveal, or opening sentences. Do not make every episode start with a morning routine and a secret investment account. Choose a fresh entry point suited to the topic.

No logo sequence, greeting, channel introduction, or demand to watch until the end before the story begins. The first useful insight should arrive within about 60–90 seconds, through the story itself.

SUSTAINING CURIOSITY FOR 30 MINUTES

- Start one central question and develop it. Do not withhold all useful information until the ending.
- Roughly every 45–90 seconds, add a new decision, observation, consequence, conversation, example, or complication. This is an editing check, not a timer for fake cliffhangers.
- Each chapter should resolve something and create the next practical or emotional problem.
- Keep at most two meaningful unanswered questions active. Pay them off.
- Return to recurring people and objects with changed meaning. Do not simply repeat their descriptions.
- Alternate events, brief reflection, dialogue, and concrete explanation.
- Avoid long runs of balances rising without anything happening in the character's life.
- A setback should change the next decision. A market decline, repair bill, job change, or family need is not just decoration.
- Use small moments of warmth and humor. Let the protagonist enjoy life as well as save money.
- The final third needs a real question of its own, such as when to spend, whom to tell, what to do with free time, or how to help someone without taking over their life.
- Build a satisfying resolution. Do not rely on envy, humiliation, fear, or a huge final balance as the only reward.

ADDING LENGTH WITHOUT FILLER

A 30-minute story needs additional events and choices. It is not a short advice script played slowly.

Each chapter needs at least two distinct story beats, a meaningful decision, and a consequence. Expand by showing a disagreement, a failed attempt, a revised plan, a change in circumstances, or the result of an earlier choice. Cut any paragraph that only repeats the message of an earlier paragraph.

Do not manufacture extra drama, delay the main point for ten minutes, add long silent shots, or slow the voice unnaturally to reach the runtime.

ENDING: CALM, CLEAR, AND EARNED

Return to the opening situation or object and show what has changed. The payoff should include a human benefit or a wiser choice, not only a number.

Financial security does not solve every problem. The character can still worry, disagree, or need other people. Let the ending offer a believable improvement and one simple idea the viewer can take into their own life.

Include at most one brief, natural channel invitation after the main payoff. An optional comment question should ask about an actual choice or value. End once, without a second summary and a long list of calls to action.

3. LENGTH AND SIX-CHAPTER STORY PLAN
============================================================

DEFAULT LENGTH

Target approximately 30 minutes. A practical planning window is 28–32 minutes, with the actual recorded audio controlling the final length.

Start with about 4,500–4,800 spoken words for a measured voice around 150–160 words per minute. Aim near 4,650 words if the voice speed is unknown. A real voice sample overrides this estimate: measured effective words per minute multiplied by 30 gives the working word budget. Include natural pauses in that measured pace.

Count actual words and characters before delivering the script. Report them outside the narration file with the estimated runtime and assumed pace. Do not claim an exact ElevenLabs price or credit cost without checking the user's current model and plan. Do not force the old short-video character budget onto the new runtime.

Use one approved, complete script for the voiceover. If the user later requests a small correction, provide the replacement passage and identify its location so they can regenerate only that part.

DEFAULT STRUCTURE

Use six named chapters or "levels" within one continuous story. Level labels may be spoken briefly, for example, "Level one. The first quiet choice." Choose fresh names that fit the episode. Do not copy the reference's six chapter names.

The following is a planning outline, not final audio timestamps:

0:00–1:15 | Opening: a familiar moment, a contradiction, and a reason to follow the story.
1:15–5:15 | Level 1: the starting pressure, an ordinary mistake or need, and the first choice.
5:15–9:30 | Level 2: the repeated habit meets real life, with an early cost or disagreement.
9:30–14:00 | Level 3: a tempting upgrade or competing need tests the plan.
14:00–18:30 | Level 4: a setback shows what has changed and what remains fragile.
18:30–23:30 | Level 5: a new option becomes available, with a relationship or work consequence.
23:30–28:30 | Level 6: the character faces the part money did not settle and makes a wiser choice.
28:30–30:00 | Closing scene: return to the opening, resolve the central question, and finish simply.

Adapt the events to the selected premise. These chapter functions are flexible; the six-part progression and approximate total runtime are the defaults. A chapter must add a different function, not replay another chapter with a larger amount of money.

The closing range includes a real final scene. It is not permission to spend ninety seconds repeating lessons or asking for subscriptions.

4. MONEY, TIMELINE, AND STORY CONSISTENCY
============================================================

Build a small continuity ledger before writing. Keep it outside the spoken script.

Track:
- Calendar year or elapsed year, protagonist age, and chapter.
- Job and take-home income, with payment frequency clearly identified.
- Main recurring expenses, debts, and affordable saving amounts.
- Cash reserve, investment balance, and other assets as separate categories.
- Changes in contributions, fees, withdrawals, and assumed growth.
- Relationship status, home, car, important object, and any event that changes them.

Make the dates and ages agree. Nine years after age 24 is age 33. Keep an object's age and the number of replacements consistent. Do not let a car, apartment, or income source change without explanation.

NUMBERS MUST SUPPORT THE STORY

- Check calculations with a calculator or code when possible. Do not copy balances or contribution figures from a reference transcript.
- A modest contribution cannot jump to a huge balance in a few years without a plausible source of growth or additional money. Extend the timeline, change the milestones, or openly establish increased income and contributions when the arithmetic requires it.
- Keep weekly, monthly, twice-monthly, and every-two-week payments distinct. If you convert them, use the appropriate frequency.
- Distinguish contributions from gains, gains from cash income, and investment balance from spendable cash or total net worth.
- A strong year does not establish a reliable annual income. Do not double-count dividends and total returns.
- State a model's starting balance, contribution schedule, time period, and growth assumptions in the production notes. Label illustrative assumptions. Use appropriately rounded spoken figures.
- Do not imply that the stock market rises every year, a loss cannot matter before selling, or every decline recovers by the next season.
- Do not claim that savings alone make work optional without showing relevant spending, other income, time horizon, and uncertainty.
- If the story includes a hypothetical retirement decision, avoid presenting one fixed withdrawal rate or one balance as a guarantee.
- If discussing the cost of keeping or replacing a car, include relevant repair and ownership costs. Do not glorify ignoring a safety issue.
- If a fictional cost is only a scene-setting detail, identify it as illustrative rather than a current market quote.

RESEARCH

Verify specific financial, legal, tax, health, or statistical claims with authoritative sources when they matter to the narration. Use primary sources where available. If you cannot verify a claim, remove unsupported precision, label the assumption, or change the story so it does not depend on that claim.

Examples of useful starting references:
- [Investor.gov index funds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-4): check explanations and risks when an episode discusses index funds.
- [Investor.gov compound interest calculator](https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator): compare a simple hypothetical growth model with clearly stated inputs. A calculation is not a prediction.

Keep research links and the numerical ledger in production notes. Put the actual sources used in the description. Do not add impressive-looking sources that the episode did not use. Keep the narration easy to follow and do not turn it into personalized financial advice.

5. VISUAL STYLE: DETAILED COLORED 2D STORY ILLUSTRATIONS
============================================================

OVERALL LOOK

Create original full-frame 16:9 illustrations with clean dark outlines, simplified expressive cartoon faces, complete human bodies, believable everyday environments, muted natural colors, soft cel shading, and gentle ambient light.

The reference feel is a warm illustrated story with more detailed rooms, cars, furniture, and objects than the characters' faces. Characters are stylized and readable. Settings feel lived in without becoming photorealistic.

Fill the canvas edge to edge. The reference screenshots contain player controls, rounded corners, borders, side panels, and subtitles as interface or editing layers. Do not reproduce those elements in generated artwork.

CHARACTER DESIGN

Use one original recurring protagonist with a stable reference sheet. A suitable default:
- Adult man in his late twenties at the story's start, unless the selected story needs another age or identity.
- Slim-to-average full body proportions, slightly large head, warm off-white stylized face and hands.
- Short dark-brown side-swept hair, simple dark eyes, readable eyebrows, small nose and mouth.
- Quiet facial expressions and natural posture, with clear changes when the story calls for them.
- Deep teal crewneck sweater, dark blue jeans, modest brown shoes, and a simple watch as the first default outfit.

Use that as a starting design, not an exact copy of another channel's mascot. If the user supplies their own character, use it instead. Supporting characters can have varied skin tones, ages, body shapes, hair, and clothes while retaining the same illustration style.

Keep recurring faces, hairstyles, and proportions consistent. Age the protagonist gradually when the timeline requires it. Specify outfit changes by scene or period. Do not let an image generator change clothing or age randomly between adjacent shots.

ENVIRONMENTS AND PROPS

- Modest kitchens, work break rooms, apartments, grocery shops, neighborhoods, cafés, dining rooms, and ordinary offices.
- Beige, cream, wood brown, muted teal, denim blue, olive, gray, and soft natural sky tones.
- Warm light for comfortable moments; cooler light for early mornings or unease, without a sudden switch to another art style.
- Clear perspective, layered foreground and background, soft drawn shadows, and a few practical details.
- Consistent layouts for recurring locations. A window or door should not jump to the other side of the room without a camera-angle explanation.
- Consistent important objects, including color, shape, condition, and ownership.
- Generic brands by default. Exact logos and technical dashboards are unnecessary unless the story needs a specific real product and accuracy has been checked.

AVOID

Black featureless silhouette characters, thin stick limbs, empty paper backgrounds as the main setting, flat neon color blocks, photorealistic people, glossy 3D, extreme anime features, excessive painterly effects, visual clutter, watermarks, player controls, browser windows, borders, rounded-frame corners, and generated subtitles.

SCENE WRITING

Show what matters in the current passage. Use a wide shot to establish a place, a medium shot for a conversation, a close-up for an object or choice, and a reaction shot when the response matters.

Do not show the protagonist standing in the middle of every image. Use hands reaching for a receipt, an empty chair after someone leaves, a phone turned face down, a partner listening, a key on a counter, or a meaningful view from a window.

Avoid repetitive split screens and constant graphs. A financial concept can usually be shown through a decision or consequence. When an exact number is essential, use one large simple label added in the editor. Do not make small generated screens carry critical arithmetic.

RESERVE SPACE FOR SUBTITLES

Compose the action so faces, hands, and important objects are not hidden behind the lower subtitle area. Keep that part of the background visually calm. The room or setting must still fill it; do not generate a blank strip or caption box.

STYLE ANCHOR: INCLUDE IN EVERY GENERATED SCENE PROMPT

"Original detailed 2D narrative cartoon illustration, full-bleed landscape 16:9, clean dark outlines, simplified expressive faces, complete natural human proportions, softly shaded muted colors, warm everyday realism, believable perspective, gently lit lived-in environment, clear foreground and background separation, consistent illustrated storybook look."

Then include the full relevant character description, outfit, age, location, action, important objects, framing, and mood. Do not rely on a character code or "same as before" alone when the prompt must work independently.

STYLE LOCK: END EVERY GENERATED IMAGE PROMPT WITH

"Keep the approved character design, location layout, and prop continuity; fill the entire canvas edge to edge; keep the lower subtitle area uncluttered but fully illustrated; no subtitles, caption text, watermark, timestamp, player controls, browser interface, borders, rounded corners, black silhouette characters, thin stick limbs, photorealism, or 3D rendering."

6. PRODUCTION DEFAULT: ILLUSTRATED STILLS WITH SLOW MOVEMENT
============================================================

Use generated illustrations with simple editor zooms and pans as the default video assets. The reference look does not require hundreds of AI-generated moving clips.

Default to illustrated scenes throughout. Do not insert real stock footage automatically. If the user later asks for stock footage or true character animation, apply that request to selected scenes and keep it clearly labeled. There is no required percentage of stock footage or generated video in this version.

SHOT LENGTH

Change to a new shot every 8–9 seconds, with 8.5 seconds as a planning midpoint. At exactly 30 minutes, that gives approximately 200–225 shots. This is a shot count, not a promise that every shot needs a new image.

Use a fresh scene image or a genuinely useful new composition. A closer view from an existing wide image is allowed if it clearly shows the next narrated detail and remains sharp. Simply restarting a zoom on the same composition does not count as a meaningful new shot.

Keep the same setting when the action continues there. A new shot can show another angle, object, or character reaction rather than a new location.

Do not change the whole image at every subtitle cue. Do not hold one unchanged composition for 25–30 seconds. A short final remainder or an unavoidable transition can depart from the target briefly; identify that exception rather than silently using a different overall pace.

SIMPLE CAMERA PRESETS

Choose one camera move per shot. Keep it subtle over the full 8–9 seconds. Use these as easy editing presets, not as actions to bake into a still-image prompt:

PUSH: Scale from 100% to about 106%, anchored on the face or important object.
PULL: Scale from about 106% to 100%, revealing the surrounding context.
DRIFT LEFT: Keep scale around 106–110% and move the crop gently left, keeping the canvas filled.
DRIFT RIGHT: Keep scale around 106–110% and move the crop gently right, keeping the canvas filled.
HOLD: A still composition for a moment that benefits from complete calm.

State the actual focal point in the scene plan: for example, "PUSH 100% to 106%, focus on the receipt in the right hand." Movement must preserve the caption area and keep faces and hands inside the frame.

Use one start keyframe and one end keyframe for each move. Choose smooth motion if the editor offers it. No shakes, whip pans, crash zooms, constant bouncing, or multiple direction changes in one shot.

Use high-resolution landscape images when available to allow a small crop. Do not over-zoom a soft image. Check that pans do not reveal an empty edge.

TRANSITIONS

Use clean cuts by default. A short, gentle dissolve may mark a time jump or a quieter change of place. Keep transitions within the scene timing so they do not add extra runtime. Do not place a transition on every image simply because an effect is available.

CHAPTER TRANSITIONS

Keep the illustration style and the story flowing through chapter boundaries. A chapter title can be a brief editor text overlay on the first scene of the chapter. If spoken, include the words in the narration and subtitles. Do not add long title cards, loud stings, or extra silent time.

8. STAGE 2: DELIVER THE COMPLETE NARRATION FIRST
============================================================

Before writing, establish the six-part story, characters, timeline, key object, and numerical assumptions. Then write the complete script without showing a long planning discussion first.

The script should be approximately 4,500–4,800 words at the default pace, adjusted to a known voice speed. Do not stop after an outline, the first chapter, or an excerpt unless that is what the user requested.

Create a UTF-8 plain-text file:
script_[topic_slug].txt

The file contains only spoken narration. Use normal paragraphs. No title heading, timestamps, bullet points, citations, visual prompts, sound cues, [music] tokens, stage directions, or production notes. If level titles are spoken, write them as plain spoken sentences within the narration.

Create a separate short production_notes_[topic_slug].txt containing:

- Chapter outline, character and location notes, timeline, and money ledger.
- Actual research sources and calculations used.

Then say:

"Record the narration, then paste the full timestamped transcript or upload its SRT/VTT file. Include the total audio duration. I will use the recording's timing to make the 8–9 second scenes and subtitles."

============================================================
9. STAGE 3: BUILD THE SCENES FROM THE REAL RECORDING
============================================================

TIMING AUTHORITY

The user's recorded narration and its transcript determine the episode's final duration and content. Do not silently rewrite words after recording or force the audio to fit the planning outline.

Read the whole supplied transcript. Accept SRT, VTT, or plain start-timestamp lines. A transcript with fewer than 20 lines is not automatically incomplete. Identify actual truncation or missing sections when present.

With SRT/VTT, preserve the supplied cue timing. With start timestamps only, use the next timestamp as the planning endpoint of the previous passage and use the total audio duration for the final endpoint.

If the final duration is missing, plan what can be planned and ask only for the missing duration before claiming a complete scene timeline or subtitle file. Never invent an audio endpoint.

Keep editing cut times separate from verified spoken-word timing. You may schedule a visual cut at 00:08.500 even if that exact number does not appear in the transcript. Label it as a planned cut, not a measured word timestamp.

BUILD AN 8–9 SECOND SHOT GRID

1. Start at the beginning of the actual audio.
2. Prefer a useful transcript boundary 8–9 seconds after the last cut when one exists.
3. Otherwise schedule a visual cut around 8.5 seconds and choose an image that fits the meaning being spoken across that point.
4. Continue through the full audio. A sentence may continue smoothly across two images.
5. Use the actual endpoint for the final shot. Redistribute the final few shot lengths where practical to avoid a tiny leftover shot.
6. Cover the complete timeline without gaps, accidental overlaps, or missing narration. Transitions occupy the existing timeline.

Do not let the many 1–3 second caption cues turn into hundreds of extra scene changes. Group their meaning into the required 8–9 second shots. If a single cue crosses a cut, mark it as continuing; do not pretend there is a precisely measured word boundary there.

SCENE IDS

Use chronological scene IDs such as S001, S002, and S003. Include start and end times in the plan. For copying and asset filenames, prefix the related prompt with both its scene ID and start time, for example:

S001 [00:00.000]
S002 [00:08.500]
S003 [00:17.000]

These are planning and filename labels only. Tell the creator once to paste the descriptive prompt after the label into Google Flow. Never generate the label or timestamp as visible text.

CHARACTER AND LOCATION REFERENCES

Before the first scene batch, give a small reference package:
- One protagonist reference prompt with full body, a clear face view, outfit, and palette.
- One short prompt for each important recurring supporting character, usually two or three.
- A concise continuity list for recurring rooms, outfits, and the key object.

These reference assets do not add time to the narration. Use them as visual references where the image tool supports it. Keep full essential character descriptions in individual prompts as well. A reference does not guarantee consistency; check the generated face, outfit, hands, and important props before reusing it.

Do not halt to ask for a character design already defined in this prompt. If the creator has an approved character sheet, reuse it instead of generating another.

EVERY SCENE MUST INCLUDE

- Scene ID, actual planned start/end, and duration.
- Narration passage or short exact cue that explains why this image appears here.
- Asset type: NEW IMAGE or REUSE IMAGE.
- Clear description of what is visible: character, action, important object, setting, and composition.
- The full Google Flow image prompt for a new image, or the exact earlier asset ID and useful new crop for a reused image.
- One camera preset with scale, direction when needed, and focal point.
- Transition instruction only when something other than a simple cut is intended.
- A subtitle-placement exception or exact editor label only when needed.

For each NEW IMAGE, give one independently usable prompt. Include the full style anchor, character and outfit details, age when relevant, room or setting, single frozen action, framing, light, important props, and full style lock.

Describe what the scene shows. Do not put the entire narration into the prompt as a caption, and do not ask a still image to depict a sequence of several actions at once.

For each REUSE IMAGE, specify the source scene ID and what different information the crop reveals. If the existing image cannot show the current action or remain sharp at that crop, provide a new image prompt instead. Reuse cannot become a substitute for illustrating the story.

BATCH FORMAT

10. SUBTITLES: CLEAN WORDS THAT FOLLOW THE VOICE
============================================================

Subtitles are required. Add them in the editor as a separate layer, after scene movement is set up. Do not bake them into Google Flow images. A zoom or pan should move the image underneath stable subtitles.

DEFAULT APPEARANCE

- White, bold, rounded or friendly sans-serif lettering.
- A dark outline or small dark shadow for readability against changing backgrounds.
- Lower-center placement, roughly 8–12% above the bottom edge.
- Around 52–64 pixels for a 1920x1080 frame as a starting size, adjusted after viewing on a small screen.
- Normally one short line, with two lines only when needed.
- Aim for roughly 3–8 words per cue and a readable phrase break. Do not force the phrase length if the timing cannot support it.
- Sentence case. No bouncing words, distracting rainbow colors, giant all-caps captions, or mandatory word-by-word animation.

TIMING AND ACCURACY

- Subtitles follow the actual spoken words and timing, independently of scene changes.
- Never hold an entire 8–9 second passage as one oversized caption just because the scene lasts that long.
- Preserve wording, names, and numbers from the recorded voice. Fix recognition errors only when the correct words are clear from the audio or the approved recorded script.
- Do not add unspoken chapter names, commentary, or [music] artifacts to dialogue subtitles.
- Use the supplied word or phrase timings. If only coarse cues are available, retain truthful cue timing instead of inventing precise word-level synchronization.
- If a cue must be split but exact timing is unknown, flag the split for audio alignment or keep the original cue. Do not manufacture a polished-looking SRT with guessed word timing.
- Do not display another scene's words early just to match the picture.

DELIVERABLE

When the complete timed transcript supports it, create subtitles_[topic_slug].srt with consecutive cue numbers, valid HH:MM:SS,mmm timestamps, and no accidental overlaps. If the user supplied valid SRT, preserve its reliable timing while applying justified text cleanup.

Include the subtitle styling recipe in production_notes_[topic_slug].txt. SRT carries text and timing, not the full font and outline styling, so provide those settings separately for the editor. A styled ASS file may also be supplied if the user requests it and their workflow supports it.

If only an untimed script is available, wait for alignment to the recorded audio. Do not claim a finished synchronized subtitle file exists.

11. MUSIC: SOFT JAZZY PIANO UNDER THE STORY
============================================================

Use a quiet instrumental jazz-piano bed throughout most of the episode, as requested by the user. It should feel warm, thoughtful, and lightly relaxed. The narration must remain the easiest thing to hear.

MUSIC BRIEF

- Mellow acoustic piano with simple chords and restrained melody.
- Around 60–85 BPM as a search preference, with an unhurried feel.
- Light upright bass or brushed percussion can be present, but piano should lead.
- No vocals, lyrics, loud horns, busy solos, dramatic drops, heavy drums, or sudden volume jumps.
- Avoid overly sad or sleepy music. The mood should support a calm, attentive story.

Suggested search phrases:
"soft jazz piano background"
"mellow piano trio instrumental"
"gentle lounge piano"
"warm jazz piano quiet"

SOURCING

Start with the YouTube Audio Library or another source whose license has been checked for the intended use. Do not copy the reference channel's soundtrack or invent a track name and license.

The [YouTube Audio Library help page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3376882?hl=en) explains its download, attribution, and YouTube monetization provisions. Check the selected track's attribution requirement and keep any required credit for the description.

If a track has actually been selected, record its title, artist, source URL, license, and required attribution in the production notes. Otherwise supply the search brief and say that a track has not yet been selected.

SIMPLE MIXING PLAN

- Put music on its own audio track below the voiceover.
- Begin with the music much quieter than the voice. Roughly 18–24 dB below the narration's average level is a starting balance, not a universal gain-slider setting.
- Listen on ordinary speakers and headphones. Reduce the music if any word becomes harder to understand.
- Fade the music in gently over about 1–2 seconds. Use a similar soft fade at the end.
- If using one looping track, choose a musically sensible loop and a smooth overlap. Do not leave a click, abrupt restart, or gap.
- If using several tracks, keep their mood and loudness similar. Change at a chapter boundary with a short crossfade rather than switching every scene.
- Briefly lower the bed further for an important quiet line if needed. Do not add loud sound effects or stings at every reveal.
- Do not stretch the video past its story ending to let the music finish. Trim and fade to the actual endpoint.

Generated animation audio, if any, should be muted. The instruction to keep generated clips silent does not remove the separate piano bed from the finished video.
